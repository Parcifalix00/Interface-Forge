# Layout and information architecture

Start with what users need to find, compare, or modify. Give the primary action a stable location and make in-progress work understandable. Do not confuse information density with disorder.

Dashboards: define hierarchy across summary, list, and detail. Every indicator needs a source, unit, time period, and update state. Do not invent KPIs to fill space.

Tables: preserve row/column comparison, headers, and actions. On narrow screens, prioritize columns with accessible detail or provide explicit local scrolling. Do not convert tables to cards when doing so destroys necessary comparison. Sorting, filters, and pagination should preserve coherent state.

Forms: group fields by task, preserve labels, keep errors close to fields, preserve entered data after errors, and make submit and feedback clear. Destructive actions need context and recovery/confirmation proportional to their impact rather than blanket confirmation prompts.

Navigation: make current location clear. Hiding a sidebar must not remove navigation. Preserve back behavior, deep links, and session restoration where already supported. Do not add routes or a new product concept just to justify a layout.

Editors/canvases: separate the content viewport from tools and state. Check zoom, panels, inspectors, and minimum sizes, including alternate access to drag-only actions. Do not impose a landing-page pattern on an operational application.

Public websites: use clear content hierarchy and verifiable CTAs. Apply SEO only where relevant and keep metadata faithful to visible content. Do not publish merely to "see if it works." A preview can remain local.
